2 · Simulation Type Selector

Evidence gathering ☐ Procedural sequencing ☐ Drag-and-drop ☐ Multiple-choice quiz
Tone / voice / reading level: Contemporary, credible, supportive academic English. Virtual panel members use natural
Nigerian English delivery if institutionally appropriate; no forced accent stereotypes. Briefings should be conversational and
concise. Never announce scores during the speech.
Core guardrail: Exactly THREE major scenes. One learner performance is scored. Topic selection is pre-scene; results and
Finish are post-scene screens. No follow-up questions, rebuttal round, practice quiz, sentence cards or additional spoken
tasks.


3 · Design Intent and Scope
The learner plays a first-year student invited to address an online academic review panel. They select one of three current
university-policy debates and have 60 seconds to present their position. The simulation tests oral communication quality,
not political agreement, essay writing, recall of facts, or ability to guess an approved answer.
One run = one selected topic. Selection is locked at the start of Scene 1. The other topics are unavailable until the panel
verdict has been delivered, results displayed, and the learner actively selects Finish Simulation. Only a completed/closed
run can return to topic selection. Restart is a new run, not a continuation.

Approved topic catalogue and full contextual briefings

T01 — SOCIAL MEDIA IN LIVE ONLINE LECTURES
Topic: Should Social Media Be Restricted During Live Online University Lectures?
Context: The university delivers teaching through live virtual classrooms and recorded online materials. Learners often
attend from home or work using laptops and phones. Some use social media while a lecture is in progress. The academic
board is considering restrictions during scheduled live sessions, while recognising that students study on personal devices
and some platforms support course discussion.
Decision boundary: The issue is not banning social media across a student’s private life. It is whether the university should
set expectations or technical restrictions specifically during live online lectures.
Legitimate argument directions: FOR: fewer distractions, greater attention, clearer class norms. AGAINST: adult learner
autonomy, accessibility and device constraints, social platforms as learning tools, difficulty enforcing restrictions remotely.
QUALIFIED: restrict non-course use during specific moments, encourage self-management, or set agreed digital etiquette.
Briefing narrator — approved draft: “Our university teaches online. During a live lecture, the same device that connects a
student to learning can also open a stream of notifications. The academic board is considering whether social media use
should be restricted during scheduled live sessions. Some argue this protects concentration; others say open-university
learners need flexibility and digital autonomy. You have sixty seconds to recommend a position. Make your case.”

T02 — AI AND ACADEMIC ASSIGNMENTS
Topic: Should University Students Be Allowed to Use AI for Assignments?
Context: The academic committee is reviewing responsible use of generative AI in coursework. Tutors report that AI may
help with research planning and language support; they also worry about submitting machine-generated work as original
scholarship. The policy must be clear and fair for remote learners.
Decision boundary: The question concerns appropriate permission and safeguards, not whether technology should exist.
Students may defend permission, prohibition in specified assessed tasks, or conditional use with disclosure.

dependency, unreliable outputs, authenticity and academic integrity. QUALIFIED: permitted for brainstorming or editing

T03 — ATTENDANCE AND FLEXIBLE LEARNING
Topic: Should Attendance at Live Online Lectures Be Compulsory?
Context: An online open university offers scheduled live webinars alongside recordings, asynchronous resources and
flexible study schedules. A proposal would make attendance at live online lectures compulsory, even though students may
balance jobs, care responsibilities, bandwidth limitations and time zones.
Decision boundary: Attendance refers to synchronous virtual lectures, not entering a physical classroom. Student
engagement and learning outcomes are separate from merely logging in.
Legitimate argument directions: FOR: live participation, accountability, immediate feedback, peer discussion. AGAINST:
conflicts with open learning access, connectivity challenges, equivalent asynchronous learning. QUALIFIED: compulsory
participation for selected interactive sessions, with accessible alternatives.
Briefing narrator — approved draft: “This university was designed for flexible online learning. Students can attend live
sessions, but many also depend on recordings because of work, family or connectivity. Management is considering
compulsory attendance at live online lectures. Supporters expect more participation; critics question whether attendance
measures learning. You have sixty seconds to advise the academic panel. Make your case.”

Topic neutrality: Do not award or deduct points for supporting a restriction, opposing it or proposing a conditional policy.
Assess relevance and logical coherence, not whether the learner matches a panel member’s preferred stance.
Evidence restraint: Do not require statistics or named sources in a one-minute speech. If a learner uses fabricated, highly
implausible or unsupportable certainty, reflect the weakness only where it affects reasoning; do not secretly run a fact trivia
test.
Duration: Recording is capped at 60.0 s. Starting speech late or stopping early is not an automatic duration penalty; assess
the captured performance only. Do not score timing separately. Silence with valid capture receives 0 across demonstrated
criteria.
Evaluation failure: If speech transcription or audio delivery analysis fails, do not fabricate a mark. Show Evaluation
unavailable, preserve the recording securely, and offer Retry evaluation (same file; no new performance) or contact
support. If no valid audio file exists due to a technical error, permit one technical re-record without marking the failed
attempt.

8 · Debrief & Leaderboard
Final score = sum of four weighted criterion points, using L/4 anchors as specified in Section 5. Example (L 3/2/4/3): 22.50
+ 15 + 25 + 11.25 = 73.75 → 74/100. Strand totals are 60/25/15. The learner receives criterion levels, evidence-linked
justification and an editable-for-facilitators assessment log. No leaderboard by default: ranking oral communication across
varied devices may create inequity and is not needed for the learning goal.

Score bands: 85–100 Strong; 70–84 Effective; 50–69 Developing; 0–49 Needs improvement. Bands are descriptive, not
pass/fail gates. No penalty for selecting a particular policy stance.
Not scored: Topic selected, learner name, number of briefing replays, time spent reading, cosmetic theme, panel facial
movement, and attempts at microphone diagnostics. Speaking duration is capped but not independently graded.
Evaluation process: 1. Validate captured audio and duration. 2. Run speech-to-text and acoustic assessment. 3. Identify
thesis/position, supporting reasoning and structure from actual transcript. 4. Rate each criterion 0–4 using fixed anchors;
audio determines delivery. 5. Save text evidence (short transcript excerpts/timestamps) and criterion justifications. 6.
Calculate weighted score. 7. Select dynamic panel wording based on grounded ratings. 8. Offer facilitator review/override.
Missing or low-confidence data: If automatic analysis cannot distinguish words sufficiently, do not claim to assess content
with confidence. Mark Needs review; do not display a made-up final score. Learner can still complete the run with Pending
facilitator review status, after receiving an honest interim response.
Backend schema (minimum): runs(run_id, learner_id, topic_id, status, created_at, briefing_complete_at, started_at,
submitted_at, finished_at, microphone_status, recording_duration, audio_uri, transcript, transcript_quality,
permission_consent); evaluations(run_id, criterion_id, level_0_to_4, weight, points, evidence_note, assessor_type,
model_version, reviewed_at); outcomes(run_id, score, band, verdict_text, strength, improvement, status).
Facilitator dashboard: Filter by topic/status/date; inspect playback and transcript only where authorised; see criterion
scores and evidence, technical failures, assessment confidence, and override history. Export aggregate anonymised rubric
results. Never expose another learner’s audio.
Privacy & retention: Ask for microphone permission and clear recording/assessment disclosure before capture. Provide
institution-configurable recording retention and deletion policy; do not promise a specific retention period without
institutional approval. Access-control raw audio, transcripts and marking logs. Do not display learner names publicly.
